주제 파악 (Finding the Main Idea) · 지문을 읽고 가장 적절한 주제를 고르세요.
유형: 영어 · 주제 찾기 난이도: 상

지문 Reading Passage

In March 1964, the murder of Kitty Genovese in New York City was widely reported at the time to have been witnessed by dozens of neighbors, none of whom intervened or called for help until it was too late. Although later journalism revealed the original reporting had exaggerated both the number of witnesses and their inaction, the case pushed two social psychologists, John Darley and Bibb Latane, to ask an uncomfortable question: does the mere presence of other people make individuals less likely to help someone in distress, rather than more likely?

To test this, Darley and Latane ran a series of experiments in which participants believed they were having a discussion over an intercom with one or several other people, one of whom then appeared to suffer a medical emergency. When a participant believed they were the only other person who could hear the emergency, the overwhelming majority intervened quickly. But as the number of other supposed bystanders increased, the rate and speed of intervention dropped sharply, even though every participant could hear the same distress. Darley and Latane called this the bystander effect, and identified diffusion of responsibility as its central mechanism: when many people share the potential duty to act, each individual feels less personally accountable, reasoning, consciously or not, that someone else will surely step in.

Subsequent research added further layers to the explanation. Pluralistic ignorance plays a role when a situation is ambiguous: bystanders glance at one another for cues, and because everyone else is outwardly calm, each person concludes that the situation must not actually be an emergency, even if privately they suspect otherwise. Evaluation apprehension also matters, since acting incorrectly in front of a crowd carries a social cost that acting alone does not. None of these mechanisms require bystanders to be indifferent or callous; ordinary, caring people can fail to act simply because the presence of others silently redistributes and dilutes a sense of personal obligation.

Understanding this dynamic has practical consequences that go well beyond the psychology laboratory. Emergency responders and safety trainers now teach a specific counter-technique: rather than shouting "somebody call an ambulance," a bystander should point at one specific individual and say, "you, call an ambulance," collapsing a diffuse group responsibility into a single, unambiguous assignment. Workplace and campus programs built on bystander-intervention research use the same insight to encourage people to interrupt harassment or hazing, precisely because they know that a crowd's silence is not proof that no one cares, but often a predictable side effect of how responsibility quietly disperses whenever a group, rather than a single individual, is watching.

문제 Question

What is the main idea of the passage?

  1. Kitty Genovese's murder in 1964 was witnessed by dozens of indifferent neighbors, none of whom bothered to call the police.
  2. The presence of other bystanders tends to reduce, rather than increase, an individual's likelihood of helping, but this diffusion of responsibility can be countered by assigning a specific person to act.
  3. Darley and Latane proved that most people are fundamentally selfish and unwilling to help strangers in an emergency.
  4. Pluralistic ignorance and evaluation apprehension are two unrelated psychological phenomena that have nothing to do with why bystanders fail to act.
  5. Emergency responders now require every bystander at the scene of an accident to call for help simultaneously to ensure someone succeeds.
