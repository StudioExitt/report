유형: 영어 · 주제 찾기 난이도: 상

지문 Reading Passage

In 1940, the linguist Benjamin Lee Whorf published a claim that would unsettle generations of scholars: that the structure of a language does not merely describe how its speakers think, but actively shapes and constrains it. Drawing on his study of Hopi, an Indigenous language of the American Southwest, Whorf argued that its grammar encoded time and causation so differently from European languages that Hopi speakers might experience reality itself in a fundamentally different way. Combined with earlier observations by his teacher Edward Sapir, this idea became known as the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis, or linguistic relativity.

The hypothesis is often presented in two versions of very different strength. The "strong" version holds that language determines thought outright, making certain concepts literally unthinkable to someone whose language lacks the words or grammar to express them. This strong form fell out of favor decades ago; critics pointed out that people routinely grasp ideas for which their native language has no ready term, and that translation between languages, however imperfect, remains possible. A far more modest "weak" version survived, proposing only that language influences habitual patterns of thought and attention without imprisoning them.

Contemporary research has lent this weaker claim considerable support, though rarely in the dramatic terms Whorf originally imagined. Speakers of languages that grammatically distinguish light blue from dark blue, for instance, have been shown in laboratory tests to discriminate between those shades slightly faster than speakers of languages with a single word for blue. Studies of languages that rely on absolute directions such as north and south, rather than relative terms like left and right, found that their speakers maintain a strikingly precise, almost compass-like sense of orientation even indoors or in unfamiliar surroundings. These effects are measurable, but they are subtle nudges in cognitive tendency, not walls around what a mind can conceive.

Skeptics caution against overreading such findings. Many of the observed effects are small, appear only under specific experimental conditions, and can sometimes be explained by cultural practice rather than grammar itself; a community that navigates by the stars because of its way of life may simply pay closer attention to direction regardless of how its language happens to be structured. Untangling cause from correlation, in other words, has proven far harder than either Whorf's original enthusiasm or his later critics' dismissals suggested.

What has emerged from nearly a century of debate is neither the vindication nor the wholesale rejection of Whorf's idea, but a considerably more careful picture. Language appears to interact with cognition much as a well-worn path interacts with a walker: it does not forbid other routes, but it makes some ways of noticing, categorizing, and remembering the world a little easier to fall into than others. That modest, empirically grounded version of linguistic relativity, rather than the dramatic claim about unthinkable thoughts, is what continues to shape research in psycholinguistics today.

문제 Question

What is the main idea of the passage?

  1. Whorf's study of Hopi grammar proved conclusively that language completely determines what its speakers are capable of thinking.
  2. While the strong version of linguistic relativity claiming that language rigidly determines thought has been largely discredited, a weaker version showing that language subtly shapes habitual cognitive tendencies has gained credible, if limited, empirical support.
  3. Modern experiments on color perception and spatial orientation have shown that language has no measurable effect on how people think or perceive the world.
  4. Cultural practices such as star navigation are entirely unrelated to language and therefore disprove any connection between grammar and cognition.
  5. Edward Sapir and Benjamin Lee Whorf collaborated directly on all of the research described in the passage, including the modern laboratory studies.
