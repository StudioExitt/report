주제 파악 (Finding the Main Idea) · 지문을 읽고 가장 적절한 주제를 고르세요.
유형: 영어 · 주제 찾기 난이도: 중

지문 Reading Passage

When wolves were reintroduced to Yellowstone National Park in 1995, after being absent from the ecosystem for roughly seventy years, biologists expected the predators to reduce the park's swollen elk population. What they did not fully anticipate was how far the effects would ripple outward, reshaping the physical landscape itself in ways that had little to do with the number of elk killed each year.

In the decades without wolves, elk had grazed river valleys and streambanks with almost no fear of predation, lingering in the open for as long as they liked. Willow and aspen saplings, a favorite elk food, were cropped down again and again before they could grow tall enough to survive. Once wolves returned, elk behavior changed even more than elk numbers did: herds began avoiding exposed valley bottoms where they could be easily cornered, and they moved more frequently instead of settling in one spot to graze. Ecologists later called this shift in prey behavior the "ecology of fear," a term describing how the mere presence of a predator can alter an entire landscape independent of how many animals it actually kills.

With grazing pressure eased in the valleys, willow and aspen stands began to recover for the first time in generations. Taller vegetation stabilized eroding riverbanks, which in turn slowed the flow of water and allowed sediment to build up rather than wash away. Beavers, which had nearly vanished from parts of the park, returned to exploit the recovering willow stands for both food and dam-building material, and their dams created new ponds and wetlands that supported fish, amphibians, and songbirds that had been scarce for decades. Even the shape of some river channels measurably changed as a result, a physical transformation researchers had not expected a predator reintroduction to trigger.

Not every scientist agrees on how large a role wolves alone played in this recovery, since the same years also brought drought cycles, changes in bison numbers, and other management shifts that could have influenced vegetation independently. Still, the Yellowstone case has become one of the most widely cited illustrations of a "trophic cascade," in which a change at the top of a food chain propagates downward through multiple layers of an ecosystem, from large herbivores to plants to soil and water systems, and eventually back up again to smaller animals that depend on the habitat those plants and waterways provide.

For conservation policy, the lesson extended well beyond wolves and elk. It became a widely cited argument that a single missing species can destabilize a web of relationships in ways that are difficult to predict in advance, and that restoring one seemingly narrow piece of an ecosystem, such as one predator, can set off changes across an entire landscape rather than affecting only the species directly involved.

문제 Question

What is the main idea of the passage?

  1. Wolves were reintroduced to Yellowstone solely to reduce the elk population, and the program succeeded only in lowering elk numbers.
  2. The return of wolves to Yellowstone changed elk behavior and, through a cascading chain of effects on vegetation, rivers, and wildlife, illustrates how restoring a single predator can reshape an entire ecosystem in unpredictable ways.
  3. Beavers were the primary cause of Yellowstone's ecological recovery, and wolves played only a minor, largely coincidental role.
  4. Scientists have conclusively proven that drought and bison populations, not wolves, were entirely responsible for the changes observed in Yellowstone after 1995.
  5. Predator reintroduction programs always produce identical outcomes regardless of the ecosystem in which they are attempted.
