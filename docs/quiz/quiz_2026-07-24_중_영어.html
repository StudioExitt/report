지문 Reading Passage

When wolves were eliminated from Yellowstone National Park in the 1920s, ecologists expected the local elk population to grow, and it did. What they did not fully anticipate was how far the consequences of that single removal would ripple outward through the rest of the ecosystem. Without wolves to hunt them or to keep them moving, elk lingered in river valleys and stream banks, grazing willow and aspen saplings down to the roots year after year. Within a few decades, the young trees that once anchored those banks had all but disappeared.

The loss of streamside trees turned out to matter far more than anyone had predicted. Riverbanks that were once held together by root systems began to erode more quickly, widening and straightening the channels. Beavers, which depend on willow for both food and dam-building material, largely vanished from the park, and with them went the small ponds and wetlands their dams had created. Songbirds that nested in the streamside thickets lost their habitat, and the cooler, shaded water that fish needed grew scarcer as the tree canopy thinned.

In 1995, wildlife managers reintroduced gray wolves to Yellowstone, and researchers began tracking what came to be called a trophic cascade: a chain of effects that starts at the top of a food web and moves downward through every level beneath it. Wolves did not need to kill enormous numbers of elk to change the landscape; their mere presence was often enough. Elk grew warier and stopped congregating for long stretches in the open river valleys where they were most exposed to predators, and this shift in behavior, known as the "ecology of fear," allowed young willows and aspens to finally take root again.

Within roughly a decade, streamside vegetation was recovering, beavers were returning to rebuild their dams, and the ponds and wetlands that follow from beaver activity were reappearing across the park. Some researchers have even argued that reintroducing wolves altered the physical course of certain rivers, since stabilized banks erode more slowly and meander less. The Yellowstone case is now cited widely, sometimes in oversimplified form, as evidence that a single species sitting at the top of a food chain can shape the physical landscape far below it, and that removing or restoring that species can set off changes that reach well beyond the animals it directly hunts.

문제 Question

What is the main idea of the passage?

  1. Beavers are the single most important species for maintaining healthy rivers and wetlands in any ecosystem.
  2. The removal and later reintroduction of wolves in Yellowstone triggered a cascade of changes, through elk behavior, that reshaped vegetation, wildlife, and even the physical rivers themselves.
  3. Wolves were reintroduced to Yellowstone in 1995 primarily to reduce the elk population through direct predation.
  4. Elk grazing has no meaningful effect on the growth of willow and aspen trees along riverbanks.
  5. Yellowstone National Park's rivers naturally change course every few decades regardless of which animal species are present.
